Lesson 3

PATRISTOCRAT

Monoalphabetic substitution — word spaces removed

Patristocrat vs. Aristocrat

  • Same monoalphabetic substitution as Aristocrat
  • Key difference: all spaces removed, ciphertext split into 5-letter groups
  • This removes the word-boundary clue — much harder to crack!
  • Still appears as DC at all competition levels
Aristocrat vs. Patristocrat
Aristo: LPM HDTZX YQJVB
Patri: LPMHD TZXYQ JVBEJ

Why 5-Letter Groups?

  • Standard cryptographic practice — hides word lengths
  • Padding: a random letter fills the last group if text length isn't a multiple of 5
  • You may see a note like "K = null" (K is the padding letter)
  • Don't try to decode the null letter — ignore it

Breaking a Patristocrat

  • Frequency analysis still works — letter frequencies don't change
  • Without word spaces, use digraph and trigraph frequencies
  • Most common English digraphs: TH, HE, IN, ER, AN, RE, ON, EN
  • Most common trigraphs: THE, AND, ING, ION, ENT
  • Look for repeated 3-letter sequences — same trigraph often = THE

Index of Coincidence

  • A statistical tool: measures how often two randomly chosen letters match
  • English text IC ≈ 0.065
  • Random (polyalphabetic) IC ≈ 0.038
  • If IC is near 0.065 → monoalphabetic → use frequency analysis
You won't need to calculate IC by hand on the test, but knowing it confirms you're dealing with a monoalphabetic cipher.

Strategy for Patristocrats

  • Step 1: Count all 26 cipher letters, rank by frequency
  • Step 2: Map top frequencies to ETAOIN…
  • Step 3: Look for repeating bigrams/trigrams in the ciphertext
  • Step 4: Try substitutions and check if real words appear
  • Step 5: Extend confirmed letters; iterate
Write your current mapping on a separate line — update it as you go.

Key Takeaways

  • Patristocrat = Aristocrat without word spaces, in 5-letter blocks
  • Harder than Aristocrat — rely on digraphs, trigraphs, and frequency
  • Null letter may pad the final group — problem will note it
  • Appears as DC at all levels
